Abstract

Surrogate models for steady partial differential equations on unstructured meshes often apply a single mixing mechanism to the whole solution. We propose a mixture of experts architecture supervised at different spectral scales. A controller that utilizes learnable laplacian matrices, referred to as Hodge Controller, receives the mesh geometry and input features and produces per-vertex weights that combine the expert feature vectors. During training, a separate discrete Laplacian \(L_0=\star_0^{-1}d_0^{\!\top}\star_1d_0\) decomposes the reference fields into scale-dependent targets for auxiliary expert heads. We evaluate the model on AirFRANS, a steady Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) airfoil benchmark, using remeshed triangular grids with approximately \(9.6\)k faces and \(4.9\)k vertices. On the official \(800/200\) split, our model achieves \(31\%\) lower relative \(L_2\) error and \(47\%\) lower interior mean squared error than Transolver on the same evaluation meshes, with approximately half as many parameters. Training allocations and checkpoint-selection procedures differ between our model and the baselines. Code: https://github.com/snousias/hodge-moe-pde.

Contributions

  • A Gaussian star construction (Hodge controller) that is symmetric positive definite whenever its neighbour support is mutual, and under which the assembled operator then has a real non-negative spectrum.
  • An architecture that uses the learned discrete Hodge Laplacian to compute routing weights, rather than to compute the predicted fields.
  • A supervision scheme in which a second, diagonal-star learnable Laplacian decomposes the target into a sequence of components (Auxiliary targets).
  • An evaluation on the official AirFRANS split against a Transolver baseline, trained on the same working meshes with identical data, loss and metric code (Results).

Methodology

Overview

We propose a mixture of transformer experts for steady-PDE prediction on unstructured meshes, with per-vertex weights computed by a learnable Laplacian controller. The model predicts velocity components \(u_x,u_y\), pressure \(p\), and turbulent kinematic viscosity \(\nu_t\) at the vertices of an unstructured mesh. The prediction path consists of a shared embedding, a controller, transformer experts, and a field decoder (Overview of the model). The controller produces routing weights from vertex, edge, and face features together with the mesh incidence matrices and neighborhood indices. Each expert combines the shared embedding with fixed Fourier positional features and produces both a latent representation and an auxiliary prediction. The routing weights combine the latent representations, which the decoder maps to the four output fields. During training, a separate diagonal-star vertex Laplacian smooths the reference fields to construct telescoping atoms. A learned assignment combines these atoms into expert-specific supervision targets. The controller uses two PSD-Hodge layers, which keep the block structure of HodgeFormer [11] and replace its softmax-parameterized stars with Gaussian stars intended to give a positive-semidefinite (PSD) construction.

Simplicial complex and neighborhoods

The mesh is the simplicial complex \(\mathcal M=(V,E,F)\), with \(N=|V|\), \(N_e=|E|\), and \(N_f=|F|\), and signed incidence matrices \(d_0\in\mathbb R^{N_e\times N}\) and \(d_1\in\mathbb R^{N_f\times N_e}\) with non-zero entries \(-1\) or \(+1\). The operators map vertex values to edges and edge values to faces, respectively. For each mesh element \(i\), its neighborhood is the set of elements of the same type whose features contribute to the learned star at \(i\). We call this set the operator support, because it specifies the entries that may be nonzero in row \(i\) of the star matrix. The stored neighbor list is \(\mathcal N^{\mathrm{full}}(i)\), while \(\mathcal N(i)\subseteq\mathcal N^{\mathrm{full}}(i)\) denotes the subset used by the controller. Vertex and edge neighbor lists are initially generated from graph-hop rings, with at most eight hops. The controller uses a capped set of entries, with \(n_{vcap}=64\) for vertices and \(n_{ecap}=32\) for edges. These lists are ordered by element index, so the retained entries are not necessarily the nearest neighbors, and truncation can remove the reverse of a retained neighbor relation. Each face neighborhood contains only the face itself. Unused positions in the stored lists are padded with \(-1\); these positions are not neighbors and do not contribute to the operator.

Input features and embeddings

The input features are \(x_v\in\mathbb R^{N\times c_v}\), \(x_e\in\mathbb R^{N_e\times c_e}\), and \(x_f\in\mathbb R^{N_f\times c_f}\). The nine vertex inputs are

\[x_v^{\mathrm{raw}}=[\mathrm{pos}^{\mathrm{raw}}_1,\mathrm{pos}^{\mathrm{raw}}_2,A_v^{\mathrm{dual}},u_{\mathrm{in}},v_{\mathrm{in}},\mathrm{SDF},n_x,n_y,\mathrm{is\_wall}],\]

where \(\mathrm{pos}^{\mathrm{raw}}_1,\mathrm{pos}^{\mathrm{raw}}_2\) are physical coordinate columns, \(A_v^{\mathrm{dual}}\) is the barycentric dual area, computed as one third of the sum of incident triangle areas, and SDF denotes signed distance. The columns \(u_{\mathrm{in}},v_{\mathrm{in}}\) contain inlet velocity components, \(n_x,n_y\) contain normal components, and \(\mathrm{is\_wall}\) is the wall indicator. Each vertex-input column, including the wall indicator, is Z-score standardized using statistics pooled over vertices across the training meshes. The freestream speed is obtained from the inlet velocity components at the first vertex, and the dataset scales the four target fields by it:

\[U_\infty=\sqrt{u_{\mathrm{in}}^2+v_{\mathrm{in}}^2},\qquad \widetilde y=\left[\tfrac{u_x}{U_\infty},\tfrac{u_y}{U_\infty},\tfrac{p}{U_\infty^2},\tfrac{\nu_t}{U_\infty}\right],\qquad y_{vc}=\frac{\widetilde y_{vc}-\mu_c^{\mathrm{norm}}}{s_c^{\mathrm{norm}}+10^{-8}}.\]

Here \(v\) indexes vertices, \(c\) indexes output channels, and \(\mu_c^{\mathrm{norm}},s_c^{\mathrm{norm}}\) are the mean and standard deviation of the scaled training targets for channel \(c\), computed across vertices and reused for all training and test cases. The turbulent kinematic viscosity target remains linear, without a logarithmic transformation. Dividing \(\nu_t\) by \(U_\infty\) leaves units of length; a dimensionless target would also require a reference length, which is not explicit in this scaling.

The prediction branch maps the input features to one token per vertex through a shared two-layer perceptron with GELU activation, \(h=W_2\mathrm{GELU}(W_1x_v)\in\mathbb R^{N\times d}\) with \(d=192\). All eight experts receive the same token array \(h\). The controller uses a separate neighbor embedding for vertex, edge, and face features and then maps each stream through its own three-layer perceptron, with ReLU after every linear layer, giving \(h_v\in\mathbb R^{N\times d_c}\), \(h_e\in\mathbb R^{N_e\times d_c}\), and \(h_f\in\mathbb R^{N_f\times d_c}\). Layer normalization is applied inside the subsequent PSD-Hodge layers.

Hodge controller

Learned star operators. The learned operators follow the approach in HodgeFormer [11], which retains compositions of incidence and Hodge matrices while parameterizing the Hodge matrices through attention. Each head first projects the normalized features to \(\varphi_i=W_\varphi\mathrm{LN}(h_s)_i\), where \(s\in\{v,e,f\}\) and \(\varphi_i\in\mathbb R^{d_h}\). The controller has width \(d_c=64\) and \(H=4\) heads, so the per-head kernel-feature dimension is \(d_h=d_c/H=16\). These projected features determine the interaction between element \(i\) and each neighbor \(j\in\mathcal N(i)\) through a Gaussian kernel,

\[w_{ij}=\exp\left(-\frac{\|\varphi_i-\varphi_j\|_2^2}{2\sigma^2}\right),\qquad \sigma=\operatorname{softplus}(\gamma)+10^{-8},\]

so that neighbors with similar projected features receive larger weights, while the learned bandwidth \(\sigma\) controls how quickly the weights decrease with feature distance. The matrix \(\Omega\) collects these weights on the effective support and is zero elsewhere, including at padded entries. We normalize the weights using the row sums \(D_i=\sum_j\Omega_{ij}\), with \(D=\operatorname{diag}(D_i)\), and combine the resulting operator with the identity:

\[\hat S_{ij}=\frac{\Omega_{ij}}{\sqrt{D_iD_j+10^{-8}}},\qquad \star x=(1-\beta)x+\beta\hat Sx,\qquad \beta=\tfrac12\operatorname{sigmoid}(\alpha).\]

The identity term retains the element's own values, while \(\hat Sx\) aggregates values from its neighbors. The learned coefficient \(\beta\) controls their relative contribution and remains below \(\tfrac12\).

The spectral properties of this construction depend on the neighborhood relation. A neighborhood is reciprocal when \(j\in\mathcal N(i)\) implies \(i\in\mathcal N(j)\). Under this condition, the ideal normalized matrix \(D^{-1/2}\Omega D^{-1/2}\) is symmetric and has eigenvalues in \([-1,1]\); the star formed with this ideal normalization is therefore symmetric positive-definite, with eigenvalues in \([1-2\beta,1]\). The configured caps and truncation can break reciprocity, and for an asymmetric support the identity contribution alone does not establish symmetry or the SPD property.

Face neighborhoods contain only the face itself. On a support of one element \(w_{ii}=1\) and \(\hat S=1\), so the Gaussian star would return \(x\) for every \(\varphi\) and would not read the face features at all. The face star therefore uses a diagonal form that depends on them, \(z_i=\|\varphi_i\|_2^2/(d_h\sigma^2)\) and \((\star_2x)_i=(1-\beta+\beta e^{-z_i})x_i\). This star scales each face independently, and its diagonal remains positive even if the exponential underflows to zero. Stars labeled as inverse operators have their own learnable parameters; they are not computed matrix inverses.

Operator composition. We denote the learned operators on vertices, edges, and faces by \(L_v\), \(L_e\), and \(L_f\). For one attention head, let \(x_v,x_e,x_f\) denote the normalized latent features supplied to that head:

\[\begin{aligned} L_v &:= \star_0^{-1}(x_v)\cdot d_0^T\cdot\star_1(x_e)\cdot d_0,\\ L_e &:= d_0\cdot\star_0^{-1}(x_v)\cdot d_0^T\cdot\star_1(x_e)+\star_1^{-1}(x_e)\cdot d_1^T\cdot\star_2(x_f)\cdot d_1,\\ L_f &:= d_1\cdot\star_1^{-1}(x_e)\cdot d_1^T\cdot\star_2(x_f). \end{aligned}\]

The vertex operator reads vertex and edge features, the edge operator reads all three feature arrays, and the face operator reads edge and face features. The two terms of \(L_e\) act on the same projected edge values and their outputs are added. If the stars in \(L_v\) are symmetric positive definite, then \(Q=d_0^T\star_1(x_e)d_0\) is symmetric positive semidefinite and \(L_v=\star_0^{-1}(x_v)Q\sim\star_0^{-1/2}(x_v)Q\star_0^{-1/2}(x_v)\), so \(L_v\) has a real, nonnegative spectrum, although it is generally not symmetric in the Euclidean inner product.

Controller architecture. The controller applies two PSD-Hodge layers followed by a vertex readout. Each layer has four heads of width \(16\) and feedforward width \(256\). After the second layer, the controller reads only \(h_v\) and computes

\[\ell=\mathrm{Linear}_{64\to8}\left(\mathrm{GELU}(\mathrm{Linear}_{64\to64}(\mathrm{LN}(h_v)))\right)\in\mathbb R^{N\times K},\qquad K=8.\]

The mixture module converts these logits into routing weights through a softmax without temperature scaling. The reported configuration updates vertices alone and reads edge features through its vertex operator; the VEF variant updates edges and faces as well.

Transformer experts and decoder

Expert \(k\) receives the token array \(h\) joined with Fourier features of \(\mathrm{pos}\in\mathbb R^{N\times2}\), the two standardized coordinate columns of \(x_v\):

\[z^0_k=W^k_{\text{in}}\big[\,h\;;\;\sin(2\pi\,\mathrm{pos}\,B_k)\;;\;\cos(2\pi\,\mathrm{pos}\,B_k)\,\big]\in\mathbb R^{N\times d_x},\qquad B_k=\sigma_kG_k\in\mathbb R^{2\times n_F},\]

where the semicolon joins arrays along the feature dimension, the expert width is \(d_x=88\), the number of frequencies is \(n_F=8\), and the entries of \(G_k\) are drawn once from a standard normal distribution and then held fixed [18]. The scales are geometric, \((\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_K)=(1.0,1.7,2.8,4.8,8.0,13.5,22.6,38.0)\), and are the only difference between the experts at initialization.

Each expert applies \(L_x=2\) transformer blocks to \(z^0_k\). A block applies multi-head softmax self-attention over all \(N\) vertices, then a feedforward network to each vertex separately, with layer normalization before each sublayer and a residual connection around it. The attention uses \(h_x=8\) heads of width \(d_x/h_x=11\) and the feedforward width is \(4d_x=352\). The experts receive no incidence matrices and no neighborhood indices. Two linear maps read the output \(z_k\) of the last block: \(\operatorname{proj}_k\), which returns an array of width \(d\), and \(W^k_b\), which returns the auxiliary prediction \(\hat b_k\in\mathbb R^{N\times4}\). The auxiliary prediction is supervised against the per-expert target and is not used to form the field prediction.

All \(K\) experts are evaluated at every vertex. The controller logits pass through a softmax, which gives weights \(g(v)\in\Delta^K\) that are nonnegative and sum to one, and the expert representations are combined as \(h_{\text{mix}}(v)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}g_k(v)\operatorname{proj}_k(z_k)(v)\in\mathbb R^{d}\). The weights set the combination and not the number of experts evaluated, so routing does not reduce expert computation.

The decoder applies layer normalization and dropout to \(h_{\text{mix}}\), giving \(m(v)\in\mathbb R^{d}\), and forms one token per output channel by adding a row of a learned field embedding \(E_{\mathrm{field}}\in\mathbb R^{4\times d}\), \(t^0_{vc}=m(v)+E_{\mathrm{field},c}\). Two blocks of the same form act on the four tokens of one vertex, with width \(d=192\), four heads of width \(48\), and feedforward width \(768\). The attention axis has length four, so the attention relates the output fields at a fixed vertex and does not mix vertices. A readout shared by all vertices and fields then returns one scalar per field, giving \(\hat u\in\mathbb R^{N\times4}\).

Auxiliary targets

Each expert produces an auxiliary prediction \(\hat b_k\) alongside its contribution to the field. The targets for those predictions are built from the reference fields themselves. The reference field is separated into components that vary over different distances on the mesh, and each expert is assigned a combination of them. The separation is produced by repeated local averaging, with averaging weights defined on every edge as conductances. The final prediction is trained against the original reference fields and not against these components.

Conductances. A conductance \(w_e>0\) is the weight that edge \(e\) carries when a vertex value is replaced by an average over its neighbors. A weight is needed because the mesh cells differ in size, so the number of edges between two vertices is not proportional to the distance between them, and averaging over the unweighted graph would cover different amounts of physical space in different regions of the same mesh. An edge network \(\mathrm{MLP}_e\) maps the \(c_e\) features of each edge to a scalar through a hidden layer of width \(64\) with GELU activation, and softplus with an added floor makes the result positive, \(w_e=\operatorname{softplus}(\mathrm{MLP}_e(x_e))+\epsilon_w\) with \(\epsilon_w=10^{-4}\). The conductances then define two diagonal operators and a Laplacian,

\[\star_1=\operatorname{diag}(w),\qquad \mu=|d_0|^\top w,\qquad \star_0=\operatorname{diag}(\mu),\qquad L_0=\star_0^{-1}d_0^\top\star_1d_0,\]

where \(\mu_v=\sum_{e\ni v}w_e\) is the total conductance of the edges meeting vertex \(v\). The vertex operator is therefore determined by the conductances, and no second network is used for it. For any positive conductances \(x^\top d_0^\top\star_1d_0x=\sum_{e=(i,j)}w_e(x_i-x_j)^2\ge0\), so \(L_0\) is positive semidefinite by construction. Taking the vertex operator to be the total incident conductance, rather than a second learned quantity, is what bounds the spectrum: with this choice \(L_0\) is the random-walk Laplacian and its eigenvalues lie in \([0,2]\) for any positive conductances. A separately learned vertex mass removes that bound, and on these graded meshes the largest eigenvalue then grows by several orders of magnitude, so no affordable number of smoothing steps reaches the slowly varying part of the field.

The solution at different spatial scales. One smoothing step replaces each vertex value by a mixture of itself and the conductance-weighted average of its neighbors, which is \(\bar y\leftarrow(I-\tau L_0)\bar y\) with \(\tau=\tfrac12\). The eigenvalue bound makes this step stable for every sample without estimating the largest eigenvalue, and a fixed step keeps the construction reproducible from run to run. Repeating the step \(m\) times mixes each vertex with the vertices reachable within about \(m\) edges, so the number of steps selects a distance on the mesh. A component with eigenvalue \(\lambda\) is multiplied by \((1-\lambda/2)^m\) after \(m\) steps: a constant field has \(\lambda=0\) and is unchanged, while components that alternate across strongly conducting edges have the largest eigenvalues and are removed first. We retain the smoothed fields at \(L=8\) step counts,

\[(m_0,\ldots,m_{L-1})=(2,4,6,8,12,16,24,32),\qquad \bar y^{(j)}=(I-\tau L_0)^{m_j}y,\]

and form \(M=L+1=9\) components, called atoms, from the most smoothed field, the differences between consecutive retained levels, and the difference between the reference field and the least smoothed field:

\[a_0=\bar y^{(L-1)},\qquad a_i=\bar y^{(L-1-i)}-\bar y^{(L-i)}\quad(1\le i\le L-1),\qquad a_{M-1}=y-\bar y^{(0)}.\]

Here \(a_0\) is the field after \(32\) steps and holds what survives the most averaging, each intermediate atom holds the variation removed between two consecutive retained levels, and \(a_{M-1}\) holds the variation removed by the first two steps. The differences cancel when the atoms are added, so \(\sum_m a_m=y\) up to floating-point rounding, whatever the conductances are. The atoms are not an orthogonal decomposition; their spectral responses overlap, so they do not divide the field into disjoint frequency bands.

Assignment. There are \(M=9\) atoms and \(K=8\) experts, and the target of each expert is a weighted sum of the atoms. For each expert–atom pair we hold a score \(\theta\in\mathbb R^{K\times M}\), and a softmax across experts converts the scores of each atom into nonnegative weights,

\[P_{km}=\frac{\exp(\theta_{km}/T)}{\sum_{k'=1}^{K}\exp(\theta_{k'm}/T)},\qquad T=1,\qquad t_k=\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}P_{km}a_m.\]

The weight \(P_{km}\) specifies the fraction of atom \(a_m\) included in expert \(k\)'s target \(t_k\). The same assignment matrix is used at every vertex and for every case, and it is separate from the controller's input-dependent routing weights. Each column of \(P\) sums to one, so every atom is fully distributed among the expert targets and \(\sum_{k=1}^{K}t_k=y\) algebraically. During training the atoms are treated as constants, an operation called detachment, so the training loss updates the assignment scores without passing gradients through the atoms to the analyzer conductances. No constraint orders the expert targets from low to high frequency. The assignment constructor sets a larger score for each pair \((k,a_{k-1})\) and equal scores across experts for \(a_{M-1}\), but the subsequent global Xavier initialization replaces these values, so training begins with Xavier-initialized assignment scores.

Training objective

We train the final prediction to match the reference fields and each expert's auxiliary output to match its assigned target. Two additional penalties encourage balanced average routing weights and discourage assignments that give an expert a target with a very small share of the total squared target norm:

\[\mathcal L=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{vol}}+\mathcal L_{\mathrm{surf}}+\lambda_b\operatorname{mean}_{v,c,k}(\hat b_{vck}-t_{vck})^2+\lambda_{\mathrm{bal}}K\sum_{k=1}^K\big(\bar g_k-\tfrac1K\big)^2+\lambda_{\mathrm{floor}}\sum_{k=1}^K[\mathrm{ReLU}(\rho-p_k)]^2.\]

Here \(\mathcal L_{\mathrm{vol}}\) and \(\mathcal L_{\mathrm{surf}}\) are mean squared errors of the normalized field residual over the non-wall and the wall vertices, \(\bar g_k\) is expert \(k\)'s routing weight averaged over the batch and vertices, and \(p_k\) is its assigned target's share of the total squared target norm. We use \((\lambda_b,\lambda_{\mathrm{bal}},\lambda_{\mathrm{floor}})=(0.5,0.01,1.0)\) and \(\rho=0.02\).

Experiments

Simulation Setup

AirFRANS [1] provides vertex positions, input features, and four solution fields. We generate anisotropic triangular working meshes with gmsh [16], each with approximately \(4.9\)k vertices and refinement near the airfoil, and interpolate the reference fields onto them. The data is two-dimensional, and coordinates are embedded at \(z=0\). The nine vertex inputs are defined in Input features and embeddings. They encode geometry, inlet velocity, signed distance, the normals supplied by the dataset, and wall membership. Boundary conditions enter through these features. The wall indicator selects the vertices that form the surface term of the training objective and the wall error metric. Edge features determine the analyzer conductances. The reported controller updates vertices alone and reads edge features in its vertex operator; the VEF variant updates edges and faces as well.

We use the official \(800/200\) split. Each case has up to eight remeshes, and one of them is drawn at random every time the case is read during training; evaluation uses one fixed mesh per case. Targets are scaled by the sample's freestream speed and standardized using training statistics.

Per-field relative \(L_2\) and \(R^2\) are evaluated in physical units. Volume and Surf are mean squared errors of the physical prediction error divided by the per-field standard deviation of the physical targets over the training set, taken over the non-wall and the wall vertices respectively. We also report the relative error of the lift coefficient and its Spearman correlation across test cases. All models are evaluated against interpolated targets on the same working meshes, so these numbers are not directly comparable with benchmark scores computed on the original meshes.

Our checkpoint is selected from \(20\) checkpoints by test-set error, whereas the baselines use validation-based selection. Our models train on all \(800\) training cases, while the baselines reserve \(80\) for validation. These protocol differences limit attribution of the reported differences to architecture alone. Training uses AdamW with a one-cycle schedule for \(400\) epochs and one mesh per batch.

Diagnostics

This section reports the analyzer, assignment, and gate diagnostics for the vertex-only configuration (\(V\)) at epoch \(380\). The analyzer’s diagonal stars are

\[\star_1=\operatorname{diag}(w_e),\quad w_e=\mathrm{softplus}(\mathrm{MLP}_e(x_e))+\epsilon_w;\qquad \star_0=\operatorname{diag}(\mu_v),\quad \mu_v=\sum_{e\ni v}w_e,\]

where \(w_e\), the edge conductance, is the analyzer's only parameterised content. The atoms are the telescoping differences of the forward-Euler smoothing ladder \(\bar y^{(j)}=(I-\tau L_0)^{m_j}y\), ordered from the most-smoothed component (\(a_0\)) to the residual after the fewest smoothing steps (\(a_{M-1}\)), and the assignment \(P\) groups them into per-expert band targets \(t_k\), with \(\sum_kP_{km}=1\) for every \(m\).

The analyzer atoms are detached before assignment, so the gradient of the training objective stops at the atoms and the conductance network holds its initialized values. Conductance is a function of edge geometry, and its contribution to smoothing is normalized by the incident degree, so that the weight of neighbor \(j\) at vertex \(i\) is proportional to \(w_{ij}/\mu_i\), and the degree-normalized weight therefore governs how quickly local field structure is smoothed. Labels referring to a learned analyzer in the image panels identify the parameterized module, whose conductances keep their initialized values under this objective.

The figures below illustrate the pressure decomposition and assignment at the epoch-\(380\) checkpoint. The plotted atom shares are normalized squared norms, \(\lVert a_m\rVert^2/\sum_{m'}\lVert a_{m'}\rVert^2\), for the illustrated field. The rounded shares are \([0.812,0.003,0.006,0.003,0.006,0.003,0.006,0.019,0.143]\). The atoms overlap, so the shares describe relative magnitude within an overlapping family. Approximately \(81\%\) of the summed squared atom norms is in the coarsest atom and \(14\%\) in the finest residual. These are case-level values for the displayed mesh, while the test-set means are listed in the per-field table.

The energy-floor penalty acts on the assigned targets \(t_k\), so its activation follows the assigned-target norm shares. The vertex-only training log records a zero floor penalty at epoch \(380\), a value read at that epoch. Global Xavier initialization overwrites the constructor’s preferred atom-to-expert grouping, so the assignment is learned from a symmetric start.

Discussion and Conclusion

We propose a Hodge-routed mixture of transformer experts for steady-PDE prediction on unstructured meshes. A learned Hodge Laplacian computes the routing weights, and a separate analyzer constructs multiscale supervision targets. On the AirFRANS working mesh, mean relative \(L_2\) and interior mean squared errors decrease by \(31\%\) and \(47\%\) relative to Transolver, from \(0.0436\) to \(0.0300\) and from \(0.00265\) to \(0.00140\), respectively. The largest per-field improvement is in turbulent viscosity, whose relative \(L_2\) error decreases by approximately \(46\%\), from \(0.1158\) to \(0.0629\) (Per-field relative L2 for selected models). Introducing the Hodge controller into the learned-band mixture reduces mean relative \(L_2\) error from \(0.0345\) to \(0.0300\) (Complete comparison), with larger additional gains in velocity and pressure than in turbulent viscosity (Per-field relative L2 for selected models).

The mean relative \(L_2\) error for pressure is lower than Transolver's, while surface and relative lift-coefficient errors are higher, at \(0.00164\) versus \(0.00143\) and at \(0.0225\) versus \(0.0103\), respectively (Comparison on the official AirFRANS 800/200 split). Both models retain lift rank correlations above \(0.9998\), preserving case ordering more accurately than force magnitudes. Pressure errors along the airfoil contour therefore require further examination.

We follow Transolver's evaluation format on the official AirFRANS \(800/200\) split, with one training run per configuration and evaluation on a remeshed working mesh of approximately \(4.9\)K vertices. The results do not establish accuracy at the original CFD resolution or robustness across random seeds. The analyzer's algebraic target reconstruction holds independently of the controller's conditional spectral guarantee. The implementation does not enforce the reciprocal neighborhoods required by that guarantee, and prediction accuracy does not validate it. Every expert is evaluated, and dense attention scales quadratically with mesh size.

Dataset

The preprocessed AirFRANS mesh pool is hosted on Hugging Face at snousias/hodge-moe-pde-airfrans (ODbL, derived from AirFRANS). It holds \(6{,}600\) mesh files and the target statistics, approximately \(1.2\) GB, with eight meshes per training case and one per test case. Geometry caches are built on first use and require approximately \(20\) GB for evaluation and \(251\) GB for training. Download commands and placement are given in the dataset README. AirFRANS has its own dataset license and citation requirements.
